step 1 The honest answer has to come first. This wreck cannot be raised inside the weather window with the assets on this ledger, and it cannot be raised with them at all. The naval architect requires 7900 t of lift capacity by T+31.8 h. The only lifting asset that arrives before the window closes is LFT-001 at 2100 t, reaching the scene at T+31.7 h with six minutes to spare, which leaves me 5800 t short.
step 2 It does not come right with more time either. Add every crane barge on this ledger together and the total is 6500 t: LFT-001 at 2100 t, LFT-002 at 2900 t and LFT-003 at 1500 t. That is still 1400 t below the architect's figure, and it would not be assembled until T+80.4 h when LFT-002 finally arrives, nearly two days after the weather has shut the site down. There is no sequencing of these three cranes that lifts this hull intact. I am not going to write a plan that counts LFT-002 and LFT-003 as if they made the window, and I am not going to put 2100 t of crane under a 7900 t hull and call it a lift.
step 3 What this ledger can usefully do is pollution control, survey and preservation of the site, and those are worth doing now rather than waiting on a lift that is not coming. The steps below are that work, followed by what I need to actually remove her.
step 4 Fix the wreck's position precisely and obtain from the owners her general arrangement, her lightship and loaded displacements, her bunker and tank plan and her cargo manifest, together with the depth of water over her and her reported attitude on the bottom. A large sunken hull is first of all a pollution risk, and the bunker figures are the most urgent numbers on that list.
step 5 Deploy AST-001 (ETA 0.7 h) and get its 280 m3/h submersible pump to the site as the first asset on scene. Its use here is not to raise anything but to control oil and water: pumping out recovered oil from a cofferdam or temporary containment, and dewatering as part of the bunker removal in step 8. It supplies no lift capacity and I have not counted it toward the 7900 t.
step 6 Survey the wreck and the surrounding bottom, and establish how any released oil is actually behaving: pooling on the surface, suspended in the water column or settled on the bottom. Match the containment to the behaviour found. Oil from a deep hull frequently does not show as a surface slick at all, and a response that only booms the surface will have missed the problem entirely.
step 7 Before any diver enters an enclosed space in the wreck and before any hot work or cutting is done on her, test each space in sequence for explosive gas first, then for oxygen, then for toxic gas, and log all three readings. No space is entered on one reading and none is entered until the full sequence reads clear. This applies with particular force here because the eventual removal method will almost certainly involve cutting.
step 8 Make the removal of her bunkers the first substantive operation, by hot tapping or by whatever the tank condition permits, with AST-001 handling the pumped product. Taking the oil off her is achievable with what I have, it removes the greater part of the risk she poses while she sits on the bottom, and it must be done before the hull is disturbed in any case.
step 9 Account for her cargo against the manifest and establish by survey what is still in her and what has already come out onto the bottom, so that the debris field is mapped and the eventual removal is not working blind.
step 10 Rank this wreck for pollution risk on a standardised method and put that assessment in front of the client alongside this plan. If she proves to be an old or abandoned hull rather than a fresh casualty, the proper engineering decision may well be to deoil her, monitor her and leave her in place, rather than to assemble the heavy lift fleet that an intact raising would require. That decision belongs to the client, but it should be taken on the risk ranking and not by default.
step 11 Keep the site monitored through the weather window and after it: oil on the surface, oil in the column and on the bottom, and any movement or settlement of the hull. If she is in sensitive habitat, plan any eventual recovery as diver-directed work rather than dredging, and weigh the two through a net environmental benefit analysis before disturbing the bottom.
step 12 Requests, which are the real content of this plan. If the client wants this hull raised intact, I need at least 5800 t of additional lift capacity on scene inside a weather window, and realistically more than that, because 7900 t is a pre-survey figure and does not appear to include any allowance for bottom suction, which on a hull of this size can add very substantially to the break-out load. In practice that means two or three further heavy lift crane barges of 3000 t and upward, or a dedicated heavy lift vessel.
step 13 Alternative request, and the one I would put forward as the more realistic: authorise a sectional removal rather than an intact lift. If the hull is cut up on the bottom and taken out in pieces, the lift requirement per pick falls to something the available cranes can genuinely handle, and LFT-001, LFT-002 and LFT-003 become useful assets across a longer campaign instead of being collectively inadequate for one impossible pick. That requires a decision from the client and from the authorities, and it requires resources this ledger does not contain.
step 14 For either route I need, and do not have: a dive team and an ROV, without which nothing in steps 6 through 9 can actually be executed and no rigging can be placed; cutting and hot work capability for the sectional option in step 13; a cargo or deck barge to receive bunkers, cargo, debris and hull sections; containment boom, of which there is none at all on this ledger despite this being a pollution casualty; and sonar capability for the submerged oil search in step 6.
step 15 Assumptions and limitations: I have no photograph of this wreck. Her depth, her attitude on the bottom, whether she is intact or already broken, and the extent of her damage are all unknown to me, and all of them bear directly on the choice between intact raising and sectional removal. I have assumed from the architect's 7900 t lift requirement that an intact raising was the intention; if she is in fact broken, or lies too deep for crane reach, then sectional removal was always the only method and the 7900 t figure was never the right requirement to plan against.
step 16 A note on the ledger for the record: it provides three crane barges and a submersible pump and nothing else. There is no dive team, no containment boom, no barge and no cutting capability on a large sunken-vessel casualty. Even if the lift arithmetic had worked, this ledger could not have executed the operation, because there is no means on it of getting rigging onto the hull. That gap is as significant as the capacity shortfall.
step 17 Recommendation to the client: do not attempt a lift at T+31.8 h. Use the window to get the oil off her under step 8, complete the survey and the risk ranking, and come back with either the additional heavy lift of step 12 or authority for the sectional removal of step 13. Attempting a 7900 t pick with a 2100 t crane would at best achieve nothing and at worst drop a partly lifted hull back onto the bottom in a worse condition than she is in now.
