step 1 Open communications with the casualty immediately and settle the two questions that matter in the first minute: is every person aboard accounted for and clear of the fire space, and which space is burning. The architect gives us 1.9 hours before the fire reaches her fuel tanks, and on a hull of this size that is no time at all.
step 2 Once the muster confirms the space is empty and the boundaries are shut, have the master release the vessel's own fixed suppression into it, gas flooding if the fire is in machinery and foam if it is on a cargo deck. That system is already aboard and needs no transit time. It must not be released while anyone is unaccounted for in the space.
step 3 Send AST-002 ahead as the first unit on scene (ETA 0.5 h). Her three crew are not a firefighting resource; their job is to establish the on-water picture before the fire tugs arrive, take thermal readings on the shell to find where the heat really is, keep watch for people in the water, and give me an accurate report of which boundary is threatened so the arriving monitors are not wasted on the wrong face.
step 4 Put FFP-002 (ETA 0.6 h) on scene first of the firefighting units and set her 7200 m3/h on the boundary between the fire and the fuel tanks. On a large casualty that boundary is the one thing that cannot be allowed to fail, and the first heavy monitor on scene belongs there.
step 5 Bring FFP-003 in next (ETA 0.7 h) and work her 7200 m3/h on the opposite face of the fire space and on the deck above it, so the space is being cooled from more than one side and the heat is not simply pushed across toward the tanks.
step 6 Add FFP-007 as she arrives (ETA 1.1 h) and lay her 7200 m3/h on the third boundary and into the space itself where there is an opening to work through, with her position assigned by AST-002's thermal readings rather than by where the smoke is thickest.
step 7 Fight the fire with those three FiFi2 tugs working together: FFP-002, FFP-003 and FFP-007 at 7200 m3/h each deliver 21600 m3/h against the 18900 m3/h the architect requires, and the last of the three is on scene at T+1.1 h with eight tenths of an hour still in hand against the 1.9 h deadline. Hold that delivery on the fire and on the tank boundary and cool the space down until the fire is knocked down and declared out.
step 8 Hold FFP-008 (ETA 1.1 h), FFP-004 (ETA 1.2 h) and FFP-005 (ETA 1.2 h) on scene as a standing relief, monitors charged but not committed. These three give a further 21600 m3/h inside the window. I want them for three reasons: a monitor can fail, the architect's 18900 m3/h is a pre-survey figure that may be revised upward once the extent of the fire is seen, and on a fire of this duration the first units will need to come off station to re-bunker and the attack cannot be allowed to drop while they do.
step 9 Keep FFP-001 and FFP-006 (both ETA 1.4 h) at the outer edge of the scene as a deeper reserve, and bring them into the attack only if the fire has spread beyond one space or if the tank boundary is being lost; that would put six FiFi2 tugs and 43200 m3/h on her.
step 10 Deploy AST-001 (ETA 1.4 h) and set its 670 m of containment boom around the casualty's down-tide and down-wind side, concentrating on the bunker-tank quarter. We are about to put many thousands of cubic metres an hour of water onto a burning ship, and that water comes off her carrying oil, foam and fire residue. The boom is pollution containment and contributes nothing to water delivery.
step 11 If the fire involves a water-soluble fuel or cargo, standard foam will be destroyed by it and the attack must be made with alcohol-resistant foam; establish this from the manifest in step 1 and pass it to the tug masters before they commit their foam, rather than discovering it when the blanket collapses.
step 12 Do not open or ventilate a space that is superheated and smouldering rather than openly burning. Cool it from outside through the boundary until the metal temperature has come down, then open under charged lines. Opening a hot space invites a backdraft, and on a vessel this size the pressure released will be considerable.
step 13 When the fire is declared out, keep FFP-002 and FFP-003 on scene for a reflash watch with monitors charged, and continue cooling the fuel tank boundary until thermal readings show the steel has come down and stays down over a sustained period.
step 14 Immediately after the fire is out, reassess the hull and her stability before standing anyone down. We will have put an enormous weight of water into her, much of it high up, and fire has weakened her structure. A large vessel saved from fire is at that moment at real risk of capsizing or sinking on the water used to save her. Sound every compartment, establish where the water has accumulated, and dewater as the architect directs.
step 15 Assumptions and limitations: no photograph is available to me, so I do not know which space is burning, how far the fire has run, or where she is venting. The choice in step 2 and the boundary assignments in steps 4 to 6 rest on the master's report and on AST-002's first-on-scene observations and must be confirmed against them before the monitors are committed.
step 16 Limitation on resources: FFP-011 (ETA 2.3 h), FFP-009 (ETA 3.3 h) and FFP-010 (ETA 7.4 h) all arrive after the fire is predicted to reach the fuel tanks and contribute nothing to the 18900 m3/h requirement. Order FFP-011 and FFP-009 forward regardless, since they will be wanted for the prolonged reflash watch and to relieve the FiFi2 tugs; FFP-010 at 7.4 h is of marginal use to this operation.
step 17 Requests: the cargo manifest and bunker plan, to confirm whether alcohol-resistant foam is required and which boundary is the tank boundary; crew muster confirmation before the fixed system is released; foam concentrate quantities, since every unit on this ledger is rated in water delivery only and the ledger tells me nothing about foam stocks; and dewatering pumps, which this ledger does not contain at all, which step 14 needs and whose absence I regard as the most serious gap in the resources given to me.
