step 1 I have to open this plan with the finding rather than with the first task, because it governs everything that follows: the refloat requested cannot be done on this tide with the assets on this ledger. The naval architect requires 150 t of bollard pull by high water at T+6.1 h. The only pulling assets that reach the casualty inside that window are TUG-001 at 35 t (ETA 0.9 h) and TUG-002 at 3 t (ETA 2.0 h), which is 38 t. That is a shortfall of 112 t, or roughly three quarters of the force needed.
step 2 Nor is this a matter of waiting a little longer for more tugs. Every pulling asset on this ledger put together comes to 95 t: TUG-001 at 35 t, TUG-003 at 51 t and the three workboats TUG-002, TUG-004 and TUG-005 at 3 t each. Even disregarding the tide entirely and waiting until TUG-003 arrives at T+8.3 h, the full fleet is still 55 t short of the architect's figure. There is no arrangement of these assets, in any order and at any time, that frees this vessel. I will not pretend otherwise by counting TUG-003 as though she arrived before high water, and I am not going to put 38 t on a hull that needs 150 t and call it an attempt.
step 3 What I will do with this ledger is stabilise the casualty, prevent the situation from getting worse, and gather the information needed to mount a proper refloat on a later tide. The steps below are that holding operation, and they are worth doing regardless of when the pulling force arrives.
step 4 Make radio contact with the casualty at once, take the master's account of the grounding, and get her displacement, her drafts now against her drafts before she grounded, her tank soundings and her cargo manifest. Confirm the crew is accounted for and that no compartment is flooding; if she is taking water the problem in front of me changes from a refloat into saving the ship where she lies.
step 5 Send TUG-001 as the first unit on scene (ETA 0.9 h). On arrival she establishes the exclusion zone, takes a full set of soundings around the hull and out along the intended escape track to map the ground under the vessel, and lands a tide gauge on the casualty's rail so that the ground reaction can be tracked against each successive tide instead of estimated. Her soundings and her report are the basis of the revised plan I am asking for.
step 6 Have TUG-001's crew work with the vessel's engineers to sound every tank and void and establish exactly where her bunkers and any hazardous cargo are, before any strain goes on the hull and before any cutting or hot work is contemplated. This has to be done now, while there is time, rather than in a rush when the pulling force finally shows up.
step 7 Deploy AST-001 (ETA 4.3 h) and lay its 620 m of containment boom as a close collar around the grounded hull, weighted toward the bunker-tank side and the down-tide quarter, so that if the hull works on the ground across the coming tides and opens a tank, the oil is held against her rather than running down onto the coast. This boom is pollution containment and supplies no bollard pull whatever; nothing in the arithmetic above improves because it is on scene.
step 8 Put TUG-002 (ETA 2.0 h) to work as the line and tender boat: running messengers, ferrying people and gear between the casualty and TUG-001, and standing by to assist. Her 3 t of bollard pull is of no consequence to the refloat and I am not going to dress it up as though it were.
step 9 Use TUG-001 through the coming high water to hold the casualty in position and prevent her being driven further up the bank by wind, swell or tidal stream. Holding ground already gained is the useful work available to me at T+6.1 h, and it is not the same thing as a refloat attempt.
step 10 Have the casualty's crew ballast or transfer as the architect directs, working away from the neutral loading point, so that ground reaction is genuinely reduced rather than the vessel merely being trimmed. This is the one lever that can bring the required pull down toward what is actually available, and it costs nothing but time, of which we have some.
step 11 Bring TUG-003 (ETA 8.3 h) forward in any case, and TUG-004 and TUG-005 with her, so that when additional force arrives the fleet on scene is as large as this ledger allows and is already rigged for a connection. TUG-003's 51 t is the single biggest increment available and she will be the mainstay of a later attempt, but she is outside this tidal window and I have not counted her toward it.
step 12 Requests, which are the substance of this plan. First and foremost I need at least 112 t of additional bollard pull on scene before the next usable high water, to bring the total to the architect's 150 t, and I would rather have 200 t than 150 t so that there is a margin against the ground reaction being worse than estimated. Two ocean or anchor-handling tugs of 80 t each, or one of 150 t, would resolve this.
step 13 Second request: beach gear. There is no ground tackle anywhere on this ledger, which is a serious omission for a grounding. A beach gear set laid astern pulls dead on the lead, holds its strain through the top of the tide without a tug having to maintain station, and is the cheapest 70 to 80 t available to a salvage master. One set would change the character of this job.
step 14 Third request: a dive team, which this ledger also lacks, to survey the bottom under the hull, establish whether this is a soft grounding that will come away on a tide or a hard one bedded on rock, confirm the extent of any bottom damage, and confirm the escape track aft is clear of obstructions.
step 15 Fourth request: the seabed type under her and the architect's friction coefficient and ground reaction, with the 150 t figure shown as a calculation rather than a conclusion. If the bottom is rock or coral the real requirement will be higher than 150 t and my shortfall is worse than stated; if it is soft mud and she is lightly set down, a revised figure might come within reach of a reinforced fleet.
step 16 Fifth request: a lightering barge and transfer pumps, neither of which is on this ledger. If additional pulling force cannot be found, taking weight out of her is the only other way to bring the required pull down to what I have, and I presently have no means of doing it.
step 17 Assumptions and limitations: I have no photograph of this casualty, so her list, her heading relative to the shoal and the condition of her plating are unknown to me and must come from TUG-001's first-on-scene report and from the dive survey I have requested. I have assumed she is not actively flooding; if she is, the priority changes immediately from refloat planning to arresting the flooding, and I have no dewatering pumps on this ledger either.
step 18 Recommendation to the client: do not attempt the refloat at T+6.1 h. Pulling 38 t against a 150 t requirement will not move her, and the attempt would risk parting gear, injuring crew and dragging the hull across the ground to no purpose. Accept the missed tide, run the holding operation above, and mount the refloat on a later high water once the additional pull in step 12 and the ground tackle in step 13 are on scene.
