\begin{frame}{The problem in one slide}

A VLM is shown a maritime casualty image, the relevant domain knowledge, and a
\textbf{named, grounded resource ledger}. It writes one prose salvage plan. A
validator then \emph{replays} that plan against the ledger.

\begin{block}{The question}
Not ``can it describe a casualty'' --- it can. \textbf{Does a plan it writes
survive contact with the resources it was actually given?}
\end{block}

Two failure directions, and the design has to separate them:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Overcommit} --- commit assets that cannot arrive in time, or that
        are rated in the wrong scalar. The plan reads well and is unexecutable.
  \item \textbf{Over-refusal} --- escalate for more resources when the ledger
        already covered the requirement.
\end{itemize}

A model that always plans boldly wins on the first and loses on the second; a
model that always escalates does the reverse. Neither is competence.

\end{frame}

% =====================================================================
\begin{frame}{One headline number}

\[
\textsc{appropriate-response} =
\begin{cases}
\textsc{plan-succeeds} & \text{if \textsc{ledger-satisfiable}}\\[2pt]
\textsc{escalate}      & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

Credit for planning when the resources were there, credit for refusing when they
were not. One number, both directions.

\medskip
\begin{columns}[t]
\column{0.52\textwidth}
\footnotesize
\textbf{\cs{plan-succeeds}} $=$ V5 $\wedge$ V1 $\wedge$ V2a $\wedge$ V3
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
  \item \textbf{V5} the right goal is attempted
  \item \textbf{V1} every named asset is on the ledger
  \item \textbf{V2a} it arrives by the deadline, in the right scalar
  \item \textbf{V3} committed capability meets the requirement
\end{itemize}

\column{0.44\textwidth}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Invariant, asserted per cell}
\[\neg\textsc{sat} \Rightarrow \neg\textsc{plan-succeeds}\]
Violation raises, and the report re-checks it and \emph{suppresses every table}
rather than printing one. A broken instrument must not emit numbers.
\end{columns}

\medskip
\small
\cs{reduce} is accepted, recorded, and kept out of the numerator: scaling the goal
down is descriptive, not a right answer.

\end{frame}

% =====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The one thing the design is organised around}

\begin{block}{The validator must not be an oracle for the planner}
If the thing that grades the plan is the thing that knows the answer, the result
is circular and measures nothing.
\end{block}

So the checks are arithmetic over the ledger, and the \emph{planner} is given
everything it needs to get it right:

\begin{itemize}
  \item named assets with explicit ratings, units, ETAs and status;
  \item the naval architect's requirement as amount $+$ unit $+$ deadline;
  \item both pre-aggregated totals --- on-scene-by-deadline \emph{and} full fleet.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A good plan is reachable from the prompt alone.} If the model fails, it
failed at something it had the information to do.

\medskip
\small\textcolor{inkfaint}{Two authoring rules make this enforceable.
\textbf{Rule 1}: assertions state facts about resources, never what to do when they
fall short. \textbf{Rule 2}: nothing may indicate which constraint binds ---
enforced structurally, by byte-identity of the two constant prompt halves across
all cells and arms, checked by test rather than by reading.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The corpus: 110 images $\times$ 4 arms}

The arm multiplier sizes \emph{on-time} capability, so the arm is a claim about
what can actually be assembled, not about fleet size on paper.

\medskip
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
arm & multiplier & satisfiable & what a correct plan does \\
\midrule
\cs{surplus}    & $3.0$  & 110/110 & plan \\
\cs{sufficient} & $1.2$  & 110/110 & plan, with little margin \\
\cs{scarce}     & $0.5$  & 0/110   & escalate \\
\cs{infeasible} & $0.25$ & 0/110   & escalate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\medskip
\raggedright\small
440 cells. Arm fidelity is \emph{exact}, not a tolerance --- a mismatch is a
generator bug, not a finding, and the report refuses to proceed on one.

By casualty state: aground 42, sunken 33, capsized 19, on\_fire 16 images.

\textbf{The \cs{overcommit} trap is deferred} (\texttt{TRAP\_FRACTION} $=0$): v1
has no cell whose fleet suffices but whose on-time total does not. The machinery
exists; the cells do not.

\end{frame}

% =====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why the extractor cannot fake V1}

Prose has to become a structured plan before it can be replayed. That step is
where a benchmark usually leaks.

\begin{block}{The LLM never produces an asset token}
\texttt{assets\_named} is \textbf{deterministic resolution} of the prose against
the known \texttt{ledger\_ids}. \texttt{commitments} is a \textbf{code-enforced
subset} of it. So \textbf{V1 is structurally immune to extraction error} --- the
extractor cannot invent an asset, because it is never asked to emit one.
\end{block}

The LLM is left exactly one judgement --- did this plan attempt the goal, escalate,
or reduce --- and that is the part being calibrated against human labels.

\medskip
Arithmetic is off the grading path entirely: any quantity the plan restates is
recorded and never graded. The validator recomputes from the IDs the plan named.

\end{frame}

% =====================================================================
\begin{frame}{First experiment: does naming the casualty help?}

Held at \cs{sufficient} --- the one arm where both failure directions are live and
margins are thin. $110$ images $\times$ $2$ conditions $=220$ generations.

\begin{columns}[t]
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{\texttt{blind}} \\
\small The domain block offers all four casualty states as ``may be''. The planner
must read the state off the image.

\column{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{\texttt{stated}} \\
\small One added line:\\
\texttt{Confirmed casualty state: <state>.}
\end{columns}

\medskip
\begin{block}{Exactly one line differs --- deliberately}
Pruning the other three states' guidance would look more natural and is wrong: it
varies disclosure \emph{and} prompt length together, so a difference could be
attributed to neither. A test asserts that deleting the inserted line reproduces
the \texttt{blind} prompt byte for byte.
\end{block}

\small The line sits in the \emph{scenario} block, not the domain block, so Rule 2's
byte-identity property survives the manipulation untouched. The condition label is
hashed into \texttt{domain\_digest}, so the two runs cannot be silently pooled even
though neither constant file moved.

\end{frame}

% =====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The test, and why it is the same test}

One arm admits no four-arm trend contrast, so the four-arm report correctly
declines to run. The paired design supplies the headline instead.

\begin{block}{Exact paired test on the discordant pairs}
With two conditions the contrast scores are $(-1,+1)$. A concordant pair
contributes $0$ under every sign flip; each discordant pair contributes $\pm 1$
with probability $\tfrac12$. The sign-flip permutation null \textbf{is}
$\mathrm{Binomial}(n_{\text{disc}},\tfrac12)$ --- so the $p$-value is computed in
closed form rather than estimated. Same test, no Monte-Carlo error.
\end{block}

\textbf{Sensitivity is stated without an assumption.} Rather than guess a
discordance rate, the report tabulates, for each possible $n_{\text{disc}}$, the
minimum $|b-c|$ reaching $p<.05$ --- and marks the observed row.

\medskip
\small A design fact worth knowing in advance: with $\leq 5$ discordant pairs, no
split is detectable at two-sided $.05$, since $2/2^{5}=.0625$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this run cannot tell us}

Stated up front rather than discovered in review.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correct refusal is untested.} It needs an unsatisfiable ledger, and
        \cs{sufficient} has none. The lean arms are the next run.
  \item \textbf{\cs{blind} is not a clean test of visual identification.} The
        requirement's unit --- bollard pull, t$\cdot$m, m$^3$/h --- already
        correlates with the casualty. This measures whether \emph{naming} the state
        changes the plan, not whether the model can see it.
  \item \textbf{\cs{over\_refusal} has a known confound.} Roughly 70\% of
        capsized/sunken cells offer no dive team or ROV, so a planner that
        correctly escalates for want of enabling kit is scored as over-refusing on
        a ledger the validator calls satisfiable --- it consults the rated scalar
        only. If \cs{over\_refusal} comes back concentrated there, that is the
        explanation, not a planner failure.
  \item \textbf{v1 scores resource-soundness, not executability.} Enough lift and
        no divers passes. This is a conceded scope limit, not a defect.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}
